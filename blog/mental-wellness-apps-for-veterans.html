Most “best mental wellness apps” lists were written for someone whose worst day involves a full inbox. This one starts somewhere else: you served, or someone you love did, and you want to know what is actually worth your time.

Reaching for any of these is not weakness. It is using the equipment. Here is what each one is, who made it, and where it stops.

The Numbers Behind This List

The VA’s 2026 National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report covers deaths through 2024. It counted 6,488 veteran suicides that year, about 17.7 a day, down from 6,662 in 2023. The number is moving the right way. It is still far too high.

62%of them had no VA care in the year they died or the year before

Sources: VA 2026 National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report; the 62% figure as reported by Stars and Stripes.

That second number is why a phone matters. A lot of veterans are not in the VA system and many will not call a hotline or sit across from a stranger. An app does not need an appointment, a referral, or a waiting room.

PTSD is part of the picture. The VA’s National Center for PTSD estimates that about 15 out of every 100 Iraq and Afghanistan veterans have PTSD in a given year, and about 29 out of 100 at some point in their lives. For Gulf War veterans the figures are about 14 and 21. For Vietnam veterans, about 5 and 10.

How We Ranked Them

Four questions, asked the same way of every option.

Question 1

Was it built for people who served?

Military culture changes what helps. A calm voice telling a veteran to be kind to themselves is not the same as something that gets why they will not call a hotline.

Question 2

Does it talk back?

A library of exercises is a tool. A conversation is a relationship. Both have a place. Know which one you are reaching for.

Question 3

Is it there when you are?

Hard nights do not keep office hours. No appointment, no waitlist, no “we’ll get back to you.”

Question 4

Is it honest about its limits?

It should say who made it, what it is not, and when to call a human.

PTSD CoachBest free VA toolkit

A free mobile app from the VA’s National Center for PTSD, developed with the Department of Defense. It gives you education about trauma and PTSD, ways to track your symptoms, and coping tools you can use in the moment. It was built for veterans and service members, and it is where we would tell most people to start.

Where it falls short: it is a toolkit. It does not hold a conversation, and it does not know you the way a person does. The VA is clear that it is not a replacement for professional care.

Source: VA National Center for PTSD

3
Mindfulness CoachBest free guided practice

Another free VA app. It guides you through mindfulness practice on your own. If meditation works for you, this is a free, veteran-made way to do it.

Where it falls short: it is self-guided. There is no one on the other end.

Source: VA National Center for PTSD

4
CPT Coach and CBT-i CoachBest alongside treatment

Two free VA apps built to sit next to real treatment. CPT Coach supports people already in Cognitive Processing Therapy: worksheets, reading assignments, and reminders. CBT-i Coach supports Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia with a sleep diary, relaxation tools, and reminders.

Where they fall short: they are meant to be used with a clinician. If you are not in treatment, they are not the place to start.

Sources: CPT Coach · CBT-i Coach

5
HeadspaceBest for focus and sleep

One of the best-known meditation apps. Headspace has offered free access to veterans and military families through its partnership with Blue Star Families, announced in November 2024. Check Headspace for current terms before you count on it.

Where it falls short: it is a general wellness app. It is not built around combat stress or military life.

Source: Headspace

6
CalmBest for winding down

A mainstream app for meditation, sleep stories, and music. Plenty of veterans use it to come down at night, and for that it does the job.

Where it falls short: it was made for the general public. It is not built around veterans or trauma, and it does not talk back.

7
Online therapy servicesBest when you need a clinician

Services such as BetterHelp and Talkspace connect you with licensed therapists by text, phone, or video. If symptoms are costing you sleep, work, or your relationships, a licensed human clinician is the right answer, and this is one way to reach one.

Where they fall short: sessions are scheduled, they are not built around military life, and the match depends on the therapist you land with. Ask whether yours has worked with military clients. Your VA benefits or insurance may open other doors, so check what you qualify for.

8
Veterans Crisis LineNot an app. First in an emergency.

Call or text 988, and veterans and service members press 1. It is staffed around the clock by people trained for this moment. In immediate danger, call 911.

Where it falls short: it is for crisis. It is not built for the long stretches of ordinary hard days in between, which is where everything above comes in.

Before You Trust Any App

  • Who made it, and do they say so? Be wary of anything that hides the maker.
  • Does it claim to treat, cure, or diagnose? An app that does is overpromising.
  • What does it do with what you tell it? Read the privacy policy before you pour your heart out. Ours is at coachjeff.ai/privacy.
  • What does it do in a crisis? A good one points you to a human, fast.
